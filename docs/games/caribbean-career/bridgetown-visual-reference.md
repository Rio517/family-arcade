# Bridgetown harbour visual basis, circa 1675

This art direction is a historically grounded reconstruction, not a claim that
an exact contemporary view of Bridgetown in 1675 survives. The selected sources
establish the port's form and role, period vessel construction, and the visual
grammar of seventeenth-century waterfront trade. Later surviving fabric and
later views are not projected backward as exact 1675 architecture.

## Authoritative basis

- [UNESCO, Historic Bridgetown and its Garrison](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1376)
  identifies Bridgetown as a seventeenth-century fortified port town at the
  northern end of the natural harbour of Carlisle Bay, with an irregular,
  serpentine settlement pattern rather than a formal grid. It also documents
  the commercial-military relationship of town, bay, Bay Street and garrison.
  The painting therefore uses a sheltered open bay, an organic waterfront and
  modest defensive works—not a monumental fantasy citadel.
- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *Portrait of the Bonetta*](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-141812)
  records a four-gun sloop built in 1673 in a drawing made about 1678. Its
  starboard-quarter study and documented sweep ports ground the small sloop's
  low hull, restrained rig and period scale.
- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *An English two-decker attacking a Barbary port,
  ca. 1675*](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-11813)
  provides a near-contemporary reference for English square-rigged hull and
  rigging proportions, ships at anchorage, and the relationship between a
  harbour entrance, town and defensive battery. The North African setting is
  not treated as Barbados topography.
- [Rijksmuseum, *A Levantine Harbour City*, c. 1660–1670](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Levantijnse-haven--73a2b696c6efe8389142230e392f8a24)
  documents the period visual vocabulary of quayside storage: warehouses,
  timber cargo containers and barrels at a working waterfront. Its Mediterranean
  architecture and depicted people are not copied into Bridgetown.

## Historical limits and representation gate

Bridgetown's port was inseparable from English colonial expansion, the sugar
economy and the trafficking and enslavement of African people. A decorative
game backdrop cannot responsibly explain that history, and must not turn
enslaved labour into anonymous atmosphere. The production image therefore has
no foreground or identifiable people, no caricature, and no anonymous labour
as scenery; at most it may contain distant, unidentifiable harbour silhouettes.
It avoids readable flags and symbols, exact claims about individual buildings,
and grand fortifications unsupported for the chosen date.
